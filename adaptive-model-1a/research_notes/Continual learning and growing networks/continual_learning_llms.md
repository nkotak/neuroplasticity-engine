# Continual learning for transformers and LLMs, and the 2025-2026 "self-improving / continually learning model" debate

Research window: 2024 through 19 November 2026. Anything older is labelled "background".

Verification tags used on every finding:
- **[fetched]** = the primary page (arXiv abstract or HTML full text, or the original blog/post) was opened and read in this session.
- **[search-summary]** = taken from a search-result summary only; the paper itself was not opened. Treat numbers with this tag as leads to re-check.
- **[news]** / **[blog]** = non-primary source, used only to locate papers or to report a publicly stated position.

All quantitative values are reported as the source states them. Many of the 2025-2026 results are author-reported preprints with no independent replication; this is flagged where relevant.

## Key Question 1: What are the main technical directions for continual learning in LLMs (2024-2026), and what are the key papers and results for each?

### Takeaway
The field has settled into roughly eight directions: continual pre-training, continual fine-tuning, PEFT isolation (per-task LoRA plus routing or merging), model editing, replay (real or synthetic), test-time training, on-policy/self-distillation updates, and non-parametric memory ("harness") adaptation. The robust empirical pattern is that replay and PEFT isolation are the dependable workhorses, classical regularisation (EWC-style) has largely been abandoned at LLM scale, model editing does not survive large sequential edit streams, and from 2025 onward the centre of gravity has moved from "update the weights without forgetting" toward "decide which carrier (weights, adapters, KV-cache, external memory) each piece of knowledge should live in".

### Cited Findings

#### 1a. Continual pre-training (CPT)
- TiC-LM (Li et al., Apple; arXiv 2504.02107, April 2025; ACL 2025) is a web-scale time-continual pretraining benchmark built from 114 Common Crawl dumps. Reported headline: an autoregressive meta-schedule combined with replay reaches held-out loss comparable to full retraining at 2.6x computational efficiency; replay is crucial to avoid forgetting on generic web data but less so on specific domains (Wikipedia, StackExchange, code docs). **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2504.02107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02107)
- A 2026 stage-based survey concludes for CPT that "Rehearsal has become a strong baseline": mixing 5-15% of prior-domain data "consistently preserves general capabilities", though "the optimal ratio remains task- and model-dependent". Architecture-based methods (adapter plug-ins, MoE expansion, LLaMA-Pro block expansion) "remain underused at the pre-training scale" because of the cost of inserting and warming up new modules. **[fetched]** — [Chen et al., arXiv 2603.12658 (v2 dated 9 Aug 2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less" (Biderman et al.; arXiv 2405.09673, submitted 15 May 2024; TMLR Aug 2024) compared LoRA with full fine-tuning on code and maths under instruction tuning (~100K pairs) and continued pretraining (20B unstructured tokens). In standard low-rank settings LoRA substantially underperforms full fine-tuning on the target domain, but better preserves out-of-domain performance and forgets less than weight decay or dropout; full fine-tuning learns weight perturbations with rank 10-100x greater than typical LoRA configurations. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2405.09673](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673)
- TFGN (Ganguli, single author; arXiv 2605.15053, 14 May 2026) claims task-free, replay-free continual pre-training across six heterogeneous domains (Prose, Python, Math, Biomedical, Chinese, JavaScript; 1B tokens per phase) at ~398M, ~739M and ~9B scale, in both From-Scratch and Retrofit regimes. Author-reported: backward transfer of -0.007 at LLaMA 3.1 8B Retrofit; HellaSwag retention 0.506 / 0.504 / 0.510; at least 99.59% L2-orthogonal gradient separation between domain pairs; "Extension A" reduces forgetting by an additional 81% at ~398M. The mechanism is described as an architectural overlay with a Read/Write decomposition (dense forward pass; cross-domain updates structured so prior-domain subspaces are not written to). The abstract gives no baseline comparison numbers; unreviewed single-author preprint. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2605.15053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15053)
- "Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting in Continual Learning through Model Growth" (Süalp and Rezaei, LMU Munich; arXiv 2509.01213, 1 Sep 2025) is the one LLM-scale study found that links **growth during pretraining** to later forgetting. Growth = depth-wise layer stacking (G_stack, growth factor 4: a quarter-depth model trained on 10B tokens, then stacked and trained to 300B tokens); 7B TinyLlama-architecture models pretrained on SlimPajama-627B (checkpoints from Du et al. 2024). After sequential full fine-tuning on three tasks with no rehearsal, forgetting (FG, higher = more forgetting) was: domain knowledge -2.8 (baseline) vs -7.8 (StackLLM); reasoning 8.2 vs 7.3; reading comprehension 11.9 vs 6.8; bias 11.0 vs -0.9. Authors: "growth-based pretraining may deliver modest improvements in resisting catastrophic forgetting"; StackLLM "consistently shows less degradation, especially in reading comprehension". Limitations stated: only one growth model tested, MMLU numbers differ from prior work, GPU/batch settings varied across tasks. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2509.01213](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01213)

#### 1b. Continual fine-tuning and instruction tuning (CFT)
- In CFT, PEFT-based isolation "has become the dominant design pattern", because fine-tuning starts from a strong base and the main risk is behavioural regression. Pure regularisation is scarce: classical importance estimators (EWC, SI, MAS) scale poorly to billion-parameter models, and the survey authors suggest regularisation is "best deployed inside architectural modules". **[fetched]** — [Chen et al., arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- Illustrative comparison in the same survey (T5-Large; Long Sequence Benchmark and SuperNatural Instructions; two task orders each): SeqLoRA shows a 21-point average-performance swing across the two LSB orders (34.48 vs 55.79); O-LoRA varies ~1.7 points (65.30 vs 67.01) and has the lowest average forgetting rate (9.13); TaSL has the best per-order AP on LSB (71.37, 73.11); EWC averages AP 30.75 and forgetting 28.41. The survey's conclusion: "no method achieves the best AP and F.Ra simultaneously" and "performance in continual fine-tuning is highly benchmark- and metric-dependent". **[fetched]** — [Chen et al., arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- "Spurious Forgetting in Continual Learning of Language Models" (Zheng et al.; arXiv 2501.13453, 23 Jan 2025; ICLR 2025) argues that much apparent forgetting in LLM continual learning is a loss of *task alignment* rather than loss of underlying knowledge; early optimisation steps on a new task disrupt previously established alignment, tied theoretically to orthogonal weight updates. A simple "Freeze" strategy (fixing bottom layers) gives substantial improvements across four continual learning scenarios. No numbers in the abstract. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2501.13453](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13453)
- "Reawakening knowledge" (Yang, Jones, Mozer, Ren; arXiv 2403.09613, 14 Mar 2024; NeurIPS 2024): when documents are presented in a fixed cyclic order during sequential fine-tuning, LLMs "exhibit anticipatory behavior, recovering from the forgetting on documents before encountering them again"; the effect emerges and becomes more robust as parameter count increases. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2403.09613](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09613)

#### 1c. Parameter-efficient approaches: per-task LoRA, merging, routing
- Method families catalogued for PEFT isolation: O-LoRA (orthogonal subspaces), SAPT (shared attention over PEFT blocks), Progressive Prompts, MixLoRA, MIGU, SwitchCIT, Multi-LoRA. The survey names **routing** as the new bottleneck: "the small footprint of PEFT modules creates a new bottleneck—routing"; poor routing turns isolation into siloed modules that do not share knowledge, and "reliable task routing at inference" is called the main open subproblem for CFT. **[fetched]** — [Chen et al., arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- "Merge before Forget" / SLAO (arXiv 2512.23017, Dec 2025; ICLR 2026 poster) treats continual learning as sequential merging into a single LoRA, initialising each new task from an orthogonal basis of earlier LoRAs with time-aware scaling; constant memory in the number of tasks. Notes that prior LoRA-merging methods (KnOTS, LoRA-LEGO) assume concurrent access to all task LoRAs. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2512.23017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23017)
- SLIM (arXiv 2410.07739, Oct 2024) routes dynamically between LoRA adapters and identity layers so that adapters can be bypassed to suppress forgetting of general capability. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2410.07739](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.07739)
- MoRAM (arXiv 2506.21035, June 2025) frames continual learning as an incrementally growing mixture of rank-1 "associative memory experts" stored outside the base weights. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2506.21035](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21035)
- In WikiBigEdit (below), plain sequential LoRA fine-tuning is competitive for roughly the first 100K edits and then degrades (especially locality and multi-hop), while LoRA-Merge (interpolating adapters with weight 0.25) starts lower but is more stable and, after ~100K edits, outperforms both plain fine-tuning and specialised editors with at most marginal forgetting. **[fetched]** — [Thede et al., arXiv 2503.05683](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05683v2)

#### 1d. Model editing / knowledge editing
- WISE (Wang et al.; arXiv 2405.14768, 23 May 2024; NeurIPS 2024) states an "impossible triangle" for lifelong editing: editing long-term memory (parameters) or working memory (retrieval over activations) cannot simultaneously deliver reliability, generalisation and locality. It adds a side parametric memory for edits, a router between main and side memory, and "knowledge sharding" of edits into distinct subspaces that are then merged. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2405.14768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14768)
- AlphaEdit (Fang et al.; arXiv 2410.02355, 3 Oct 2024; ICLR 2025 Oral) projects the edit perturbation onto the null space of preserved knowledge before applying it; reported to improve most locate-then-edit methods by an average of 36.7% across LLaMA3, GPT2-XL and GPT-J, with one extra line of code. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2410.02355](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02355)
- "The Mirage of Model Editing" (Yang et al.; arXiv 2502.11177, 16 Feb 2025; ACL 2025 main): under a realistic QA evaluation (QAEdit benchmark, WILD framework) single-edit success is 38.5% versus 96.8% reported previously; the gap is attributed chiefly to teacher forcing at test time, which leaks ground-truth content and length. In sequential editing, current approaches "fail drastically with only 1000 edits". **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2502.11177](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11177)
- WikiBigEdit (Thede, Roth, Bethge, Akata, Hartvigsen; arXiv 2503.05683, 7 Mar 2025; ICML 2025): 502,382 QA pairs from real Wikidata edits over eight timesteps (Feb-July 2024); five 7-8B models (Llama-2-7b, Llama-3-8b, Mistral-7b, Gemma-7b, xGen-7b). ROME, R-ROME and MEMIT accuracy "rapidly degrades within the first few hundred updates, leading to model collapse"; WISE avoids collapse but declines over the first 10K updates and "Even methods designed for lifelong editing converge to pre-update performance early". RAG "vastly outperforms specialized knowledge editing techniques (at higher inference cost)", with average accuracy 78.64% (rephrased) and 66.16% (persona) and inference time at most doubling; its weakness is multi-hop reasoning (only ~10% of multi-hop samples retrieve both facts). **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2503.05683](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05683v2)
- A 2026 reproducibility study of AlphaEdit (arXiv 2606.26783, June 2026) reportedly reproduces the original metrics under the original setup but finds the advantage does not generalise uniformly to newer model families, and that large-scale sequential editing degrades downstream competence and safety-relevant refusal behaviour. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2606.26783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26783)
- A January 2026 spectral analysis (arXiv 2601.11042) ties sequential-editing collapse to disruption of dominant singular directions of the edited weight matrices and reports a mitigation that holds to 20,000 edits. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2601.11042](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11042)

#### 1e. Replay and rehearsal, including synthetic and self-generated data
- SuRe (Hazard, Fountas et al., Huawei Noah's Ark; arXiv 2511.22367, 27 Nov 2025) attributes replay's lag behind multi-task learning in LLMs to two failures: *selection* (what to rehearse) and *integration* (how to consolidate). It stores the most surprising (highest negative log-likelihood) sequences and pairs this with a dual learner: fast and slow LoRA adapters, the slow one merged by exponential moving average. Reported: state of the art on the Large Number of Tasks (LNT) benchmark, best average across Standard CL and LNT, up to +5 accuracy points on LNT over prior SOTA when combined with the dual learner; robust to reduced replay frequency and small buffers. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2511.22367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.22367)
- Survey assessment: providing historical data at training time is "the most reliable forgetting-mitigation strategy currently available", but replay faces "the twin burdens of storage and privacy", and synthetic/pseudo-replay is "insufficiently faithful" in open-ended generation. Pseudo-sample approaches (e.g. SSR, which uses the model's own in-context learning to generate rehearsal data, "eliminat[ing] the need to store real training samples") are the main privacy workaround. **[fetched]** — [Chen et al., arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- Lampinen et al. (Google DeepMind; arXiv 2505.00661, May 2025; v3 Nov 2025) show that fine-tuning generalisation can be improved, and made better than in-context learning on the original data, by spending train-time compute to augment the fine-tuning set with in-context inferences. This is the cleanest evidence that *synthetic self-generated data* is what makes weight updates generalise. **[search-summary of abstract]** — [arXiv 2505.00661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)

#### 1f. Test-time training (TTT) and inference-time adaptation
- "The Surprising Effectiveness of Test-Time Training for Few-Shot Learning" (Akyürek, Damani, Zweiger et al., MIT; arXiv 2411.07279, 11 Nov 2024): temporarily updating parameters at inference on a loss derived from the in-context examples gives up to 6x higher accuracy than fine-tuned baselines on ARC; an 8B model reaches 53.0% on the ARC public validation set, and 61.9% when ensembled with program synthesis (described as matching average human performance). On BIG-Bench Hard (10-shot), TTT improves over standard few-shot prompting by 7.3 points (50.5% to 57.8%). **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2411.07279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07279)
- Titans (Behrouz, Zhong, Mirrokni, Google; arXiv 2501.00663, 31 Dec 2024) adds a neural long-term memory module that learns to memorise at test time while attention acts as short-term memory; claimed to scale to context windows larger than 2M tokens with higher needle-in-a-haystack accuracy than baselines. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2501.00663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00663)
- The August 2026 survey "Continual Learning in Transition" treats inference-time methods as a shift "from static deployment to persistent adaptation", citing TTT-LM (context compressed into fast weights) and TTRL (reward derived from the model's own sampled outputs on unlabeled test inputs), and presents TTT as the answer to a structural ceiling on context-only learning. **[fetched]** — [Hou et al., arXiv 2608.06216](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06216)

#### 1g. On-policy and self-distillation updates (new in 2026)
- "Self-Distillation Enables Continual Learning" (Shenfeld, Damani, Hübotter, Agrawal, MIT; arXiv 2601.19897, 27 Jan 2026; v2 7 Aug 2026). SDFT: the same model is teacher (sees query + demonstration) and student (sees only the query); the student trains on its own rollouts against a per-token KL to an EMA teacher. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, new-task accuracy / average prior-capability score (base prior = 65.5): Science Q&A — SFT 66.2 / 53.4, SDFT 70.2 / 64.5; Tool Use — SFT 63.2 / 56.0, SDFT 70.6 / 65.4; Medical — SFT 35.5 / 60.2, SDFT 40.2 / 65.4. In sequential learning of three skills, SDFT accumulates all three without regression while SFT oscillates (figure only). Stated limitations: ~2.5x the FLOPs and ~4x the wall-clock of SFT; depends on strong in-context learning (at 3B, SDFT trails SFT); some residual forgetting remains. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2601.19897](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19897)
- The transition survey summarises the argument that "on-policy RL implicitly prefers, among the many solutions to a new task, the one with smallest KL" from the current policy, which is why on-policy updates forget less than off-policy SFT. **[fetched]** — [Hou et al., arXiv 2608.06216](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06216)

#### 1h. Memory and retrieval systems used instead of weight updates
- "Harness Continual Learning" (Kang et al.; arXiv 2608.19013, 19 Aug 2026) formalises the paradigm in which the foundation model is frozen and its harness (prompts, memories, tools, skills, routing rules) evolves; it defines "harness-level forgetting" and separates a Continual Optimizer (proposes harness updates) from a Continual Evaluator (commits only after checking current improvement, historical retention and validity). Reports relative gains above 10% over corresponding baselines in multiple settings and measurable harness-level forgetting with a tunable stability-plasticity trade-off. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2608.19013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19013)
- "When Continual Learning Moves to Memory" (Hu, Long, Wang, NTU; arXiv 2604.27003, 29 Apr 2026) studies a frozen Qwen-Plus ReAct agent with external memory on sequential task pairs in ALFWorld and BabyAI. Raw-trajectory memory gave *negative* forward transfer (FWT -9.5 ALFWorld, -7.5 BabyAI) while abstracted "insight" memory gave +6.5 and +9.0. The per-insight storage condition with the strongest forward transfer (+15.0 FWT) also showed BWT of -10.0, attributed to memory dilution: "Old memories are not erased but become harder to access as the pool grows." In one analysis 88.5% of homogeneous Task B queries retrieved the same top-1 item ("retrieval diversity collapse"). Limitations: two environments, one task pair each, one sparse retriever, two runs per condition. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2604.27003](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27003)
- Cartridges (arXiv 2506.06266, June 2025; ICLR 2026) trains a KV-cache representation of a corpus offline via "self-study"; reported to match ICL performance with 38.6x less memory and 26.4x higher throughput on long-context benchmarks. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2506.06266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266)
- Doc-to-LoRA (Charakorn, Cetin, Uesaka, Lange, Sakana AI; arXiv 2602.15902, 13 Feb 2026): a hypernetwork that meta-learns approximate context distillation in a single forward pass, emitting a LoRA for an unseen document; reports near-perfect zero-shot needle-in-a-haystack accuracy at lengths more than 4x the target model's native context window. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2602.15902](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15902)
- Other 2026 memory-side entries located but not opened: "Forget to Improve" (budget-curated on-device agent memory; arXiv 2606.25115), JitRL (training-free test-time policy optimisation from a trajectory memory bank; arXiv 2601.18510), "Continual Knowledge Updating in LLM Systems: Learning Through Multi-Timescale Memory Dynamics" (ICML 2026 workshop; arXiv 2605.05097). **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2606.25115](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.25115v1); [arXiv 2601.18510](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18510); [arXiv 2605.05097](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.05097)

### Inferences
- The method families that reliably work at LLM scale are the ones that either (a) re-present old data (replay, 5-15% mixing) or (b) do not touch the old parameters at all (frozen base + adapter/side memory/KV-cache/external store). Methods that try to *protect* shared weights analytically (EWC-style importance, locate-then-edit) are the ones the 2025-2026 literature reports as failing at scale.
- Growth is present in LLM continual learning almost exclusively as *module addition* (new LoRA, new expert, new side memory, new block). Neuron-level grow-and-prune inside a shared network is essentially absent from the LLM literature located here; the only growth-vs-forgetting result at LLM scale (Süalp and Rezaei) concerns growth during *pretraining*, not growth as a response to new tasks, and its effect is described by its authors as modest.
- "Routing is the bottleneck" is a recurring finding in three separate places (PEFT routing, WISE's router, retrieval in memory agents). Any architecture that adds capacity per task inherits the question of how capacity is selected at inference without task labels.
- The spurious-forgetting result implies that a small experiment should distinguish *knowledge loss* from *readout/alignment loss* (for example by probing or brief re-alignment) before attributing a drop to catastrophic forgetting.

### Gaps
- TiC-LM, Lampinen et al. (2505.00661), Cartridges, SLAO, SLIM, MoRAM and the 2026 editing follow-ups (2606.26783, 2601.11042) were not opened; their numbers above are from search summaries and should be re-verified before being quoted in a final report.
- I did not find a 2024-2026 head-to-head benchmark that runs all eight directions on a common task stream; comparisons are pairwise and benchmark-specific.
- Background works not verified in this session and therefore not cited as findings: O-LoRA (reported as arXiv 2310.14152, 2023), TRACE benchmark (2023), and Dohare et al., "Loss of plasticity in deep continual learning" (Nature, 2024; continual backpropagation re-initialises a small fraction of low-utility units). The Nature page redirected to a login wall, so its details could not be confirmed here. This last paper is directly relevant to grow/prune positioning and should be checked by whoever covers the growing-networks topic.
- No verified 2024-2026 result was found on classical regularisation methods (EWC/SI) *succeeding* at LLM scale; the survey statement that they "scale poorly" is the only evidence recorded.

## Key Question 2: What prominent new theories or framings appeared in 2025-2026, what do they claim, and what evidence supports them?

### Takeaway
Three framings dominate: (1) Google's Nested Learning / HOPE and its "Sleep" follow-up, which recast a model as nested optimisation problems updating at different frequencies and add explicit consolidation; (2) MIT's self-adaptation line (TTT, SEAL, SDFT), in which the model generates its own training signal; and (3) a system-level reframing ("Continual Learning in Transition", "Harness Continual Learning") in which weights are only one of several carriers of learned state. The evidence for (1) and (2) is author-reported, small-scale and partly figure-only, and both SEAL and Sleep explicitly report that forgetting is not solved.

### Cited Findings

#### Nested Learning and HOPE (Google Research)
- "Nested Learning: The Illusion of Deep Learning Architectures" (Behrouz, Razaviyayn, Zhong, Mirrokni; NeurIPS 2025; arXiv 2512.24695, submitted 31 Dec 2025). Claims: a model is a set of nested, multi-level and/or parallel optimisation problems, each with its own "context flow"; existing deep learning methods learn by compressing their own context flow; in-context learning emerges naturally in large models; gradient-based optimisers such as Adam and SGD with momentum are associative memory modules that compress gradient information; the paper proposes more expressive optimisers, a self-modifying sequence model that learns its own update algorithm, and a Continuum Memory System (CMS) generalising the long-term/short-term memory split. Combining these yields "Hope", for which the abstract claims "promising results" in language modeling, knowledge incorporation, few-shot generalisation, continual learning and long-context reasoning. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2512.24695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24695)
- The Google Research blog post (7 Nov 2025) describes HOPE as a self-modifying recurrent architecture, a variant of Titans augmented with CMS blocks; Titans has two levels of parameter updates, whereas HOPE is said to allow unbounded levels of in-context learning. It frames current LLMs via an anterograde-amnesia analogy: knowledge is confined to the immediate context window or to static pretraining. The blog reports comparisons against Titans, Samba and a Transformer baseline on language modeling and common-sense reasoning (lower perplexity, higher accuracy) and against Titans, TTT and Mamba2 on needle-in-a-haystack variants, but gives **no numerical scores** and no continual-learning results. **[fetched; blog]** — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/introducing-nested-learning-a-new-ml-paradigm-for-continual-learning/)
- Reception, as reported by a news outlet (Transformer, 22 Jan 2026): lead author Ali Behrouz, asked whether the results lead to "the nirvana of continual learning", said "It might be the case that what I've explained here is enough…but I doubt that"; Bing Liu (UIC) called Nested Learning "not a breakthrough" and probably "just making the model forget a little bit less"; roboticist Chris Paxton said HOPE, at 760M-1.3B parameters, still seems "far from use on anything more than toy tasks". **[news]** — [Transformer, "Teaching AI to learn"](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/teaching-ai-to-continual-learning)
- Reproduction status: an unofficial PyTorch reimplementation on Hugging Face states that no official code or weights exist and that it was run at reduced scale; another open-source reproduction says it targets mechanism-auditing fidelity rather than paper-scale results parity. No published independent replication of the continual-learning numbers was found. **[search-summary]** — [HF: smallhours19/nested-learning-hope](https://huggingface.co/smallhours19/nested-learning-hope)

#### Sleep / consolidation
- "Language Models Need Sleep: Learning to Self-Modify and Consolidate Memories" (Behrouz, Hashemi, Mirrokni — the search listing additionally names Adel Javanmard; arXiv 2606.03979, 2 Jun 2026; on OpenReview since Sept 2025). Two stages: (i) **Memory Consolidation / Knowledge Seeding** — "upward distillation" from faster, smaller memory blocks into slower, larger ones using on-policy distillation plus an imitation-learning reward; (ii) **Dreaming** — the model generates synthetic data, selects top-k by gradient-based importance plus random samples, and is rewarded when a LoRA-fine-tuned copy improves (builds on SEAL, optimised with ReSTEM-EM). **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2606.03979](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03979v1)
- Directly relevant to grow-and-prune: the Sleep method performs **parameter expansion before consolidation** (a low-rank expert is added to the slower MLP block, which is a sparse mixture of experts) and afterwards **resets the low-rank parameters from earlier sleep periods**, which the authors compare to synaptic pruning. Ablation on Qwen3-8B (AIME-24 / AIME-25 / HMMT-25, average@16): full Sleep 79.2 / 69.0 / 46.1; without Expansion 78.2 / 67.9 / 44.9; without imitation learning 76.8 / 67.9 / 45.0. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2606.03979](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03979v1)
- Sleep results with numbers: SQuAD no-context knowledge incorporation — base 31.9; SEAL 46.7 (n=1) / 43.2 (n=200); Sleep (Transformer) 48.1 / 44.3; Sleep (four-level) 48.9 / 46.2; without Dreaming 35.7 / 36.2. ARC subset (8 held-out tasks): ICL 0%, TTT 10%, SEAL 72.5%, Sleep 80%. Maths (Qwen3-8B): base 73.8 / 68.1 / 42.4, SFT 75.5 / 66.4 / 43.7, GRPO 76.4 / 68.1 / 44.9, OPSD 76.6 / 67.4 / 45.1, Sleep 79.2 / 69.0 / 46.1. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2606.03979](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03979v1)
- The actual continual-learning evidence in the Sleep paper (class-incremental CLINC, Banking, DBpedia versus ICL, EWC, InCA; continual learning of Manchu and Kalamang in context; long-context MK-NIAH, LongHealth, QASPER; BABILong to 10M tokens) is presented **in figures only**, with no tabulated numbers in the portion read. The authors themselves flag "the risk of iteratively applying self-improvement in continual learning setup" as a possible cause of catastrophic forgetting. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2606.03979](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03979v1)
- SCM, "Sleep-Consolidated Memory with Algorithmic Forgetting" (arXiv 2604.20943, April 2026) is a memory-architecture variant with offline NREM consolidation, REM dreaming and intentional forgetting phases. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2604.20943](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.20943)

#### Self-adapting / self-editing models
- SEAL, "Self-Adapting Language Models" (Zweiger, Pari, Guo, Akyürek, Kim, Agrawal, MIT; arXiv 2506.10943, 12 Jun 2025; v2 18 Sep 2025; NeurIPS 2025). The model emits "self-edits" (natural-language directives specifying fine-tuning data and optionally hyperparameters); an outer RL loop (ReSTEM) rewards self-edits by the downstream performance of the updated model. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2506.10943](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2)
- SEAL knowledge incorporation (Qwen2.5-7B, SQuAD no-context accuracy %; single passage LoRA / CPT n=200 / CPT n=2067): base 32.7 / 32.7 / 29.0; train on passage only 33.5 / 36.0 / 31.2; passage + base-model synthetic data 39.7 / 50.6 / 43.4; passage + GPT-4.1 synthetic data 46.3 / 59.4 / 49.2; SEAL 47.0 / 58.2 / 46.4. Note that SEAL beats GPT-4.1 synthetic data only in the single-passage setting; GPT-4.1 data is ahead at n=200 and n=2067. Two ReSTEM rounds (batch of 50 contexts, 5 self-edits each). **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2506.10943](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2)
- SEAL few-shot (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, curated ARC subset, 8 held-out tasks x 5 self-edits): ICL 0%; TTT + self-edit without prior RL 20%; SEAL 72.5%; Oracle TTT 100%. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2506.10943](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2)
- SEAL's stated limitations: under repeated self-edits "performance on earlier tasks gradually declines as the number of edits increases" (though it "can perform multiple updates without complete collapse"); "each self-edit evaluation takes approximately 30–45 seconds"; and reward computation is coupled to a labelled downstream task, which "prevents RL training of SEAL from scaling to unlabeled corpora". **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2506.10943](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2)
- SDFT (see 1g) is the 2026 successor from the same group: on a knowledge-acquisition task (~200K tokens of Wikipedia on 2025 natural disasters; strict / lenient / OOD accuracy) — Oracle RAG 91 / 100 / 100; continued pretraining 9 / 37 / 7; SFT 80 / 95 / 80; SDFT 89 / 100 / 98. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2601.19897](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19897)

#### Fast and slow weights / multi-timescale memory
- Fast/slow separation recurs in three independent 2025-2026 designs: the Continuum Memory System (memory modules updating at different frequencies) in Nested Learning; SuRe's fast and slow LoRA adapters with EMA consolidation; and SDFT's EMA teacher. **[fetched]** — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/introducing-nested-learning-a-new-ml-paradigm-for-continual-learning/); [arXiv 2511.22367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.22367); [arXiv 2601.19897](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19897)

#### System-level reframings
- "Continual Learning in Transition" (Hou, Zhang, Feng, Wang et al., incl. Tat-Seng Chua; arXiv 2608.06216, 6 Aug 2026) argues the field is moving "from parameter-centric learning toward system-level adaptation" and organises it along three axes: **When** (pre-training, post-training, inference time), **How** (off-policy, on-policy, beyond-gradient optimisation) and **Where** (internal parameters versus external structural components: memory, skill libraries, interaction protocols). **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2608.06216](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06216)
- "LLM Evolution as an Industry-Scale Ecosystem" (Jiang et al.; arXiv 2606.24901, 12 Jun 2026) frames industrial continual learning as a closed-loop update-and-release problem in a versioned ecosystem and names three challenges: repeated adaptation erodes plasticity; foundation-model upgrades break capability inheritance; long-term sustainability is constrained by deployment requirements. First of its five design principles is "preserving plasticity headroom". **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2606.24901](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24901)
- "Latent learning" (Lampinen, Engelcke, Li, Chaudhry, McClelland, Google DeepMind; arXiv 2509.16189, 19 Sep 2025) argues parametric learners fail to learn information not relevant to the current task but useful later (linked to the reversal curse), and that episodic memory with retrieval complements parametric learning; a system with oracle retrieval uses past experiences more flexibly, and within-example in-context learning matters for using retrieved examples. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2509.16189](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16189)

### Inferences
- The newest framings converge on the same structure from different directions: a fast, cheap, volatile store plus a slow, expensive, stable store, with an explicit *consolidation* operation between them (CMS frequencies; sleep/"upward distillation"; fast/slow LoRA with EMA; context then SDFT; harness then weights). The unsolved design question is the consolidation policy: what to move, when, and into which capacity.
- The Sleep paper is the closest published LLM-scale analogue to a grow-then-prune cycle (expand capacity before consolidation, reset/prune afterwards), and its ablation attributes roughly one point of maths accuracy to the expansion step. That is a small, author-reported effect on a reasoning benchmark, not a forgetting measurement, so it supports the plausibility of grow/prune as a consolidation mechanism but is not evidence that it reduces forgetting.
- SEAL and Sleep are better described as *learned data-generation for a standard fine-tuning update* than as new learning rules; both inherit ordinary forgetting from the inner fine-tuning step, which is exactly what their limitations sections report.
- Nested Learning's central theoretical claims (optimisers as associative memories, architecture and optimiser as the same object at different update frequencies) are reinterpretations; the empirical case rests on HOPE, for which I found no independent replication and for which the blog gives no numbers.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full Nested Learning paper (only the arXiv abstract and the Google blog), so HOPE's actual continual-learning tables, model sizes and baselines are not recorded here. The 760M-1.3B size comes from a quoted third party in a news article.
- The Sleep paper's class-incremental and continual language-learning results are figure-only in the text read; exact numbers were not recoverable. The final ~40% of the HTML (appendices, efficiency analysis) was not read.
- Authorship discrepancy for arXiv 2606.03979 (three versus four authors) between the HTML page and the search listing was not resolved.
- I found no primary source for a "self-distillation loop" framing beyond SDFT, and no peer-reviewed critique of SEAL; critical commentary located was informal.
- OPSD and GRPO appear as baselines in the Sleep paper and in the transition survey, but I did not open their primary papers.

## Key Question 3: What do recent surveys of continual learning for LLMs conclude about what works, what does not, and what is unsolved?

### Takeaway
Surveys agree that replay and PEFT-based isolation work, that classical regularisation and model editing do not scale, and that evaluation is the weakest link (short task sequences, static benchmarks, forward transfer rarely reported). The 2026 surveys add that forgetting now has several "faces" across weights, memory, context and skills, and that nobody has a principled rule for when to consolidate knowledge into parameters versus leave it in external state.

### Cited Findings
- Shi et al., "Continual Learning of Large Language Models: A Comprehensive Survey" (arXiv 2404.16789; submitted 25 Apr 2024, v3 25 Nov 2024; marked "Work in progress" on arXiv). Organises the field by **vertical continuity** (general to specific capability) and **horizontal continuity** (across time and domains), over three stages: Continual Pre-Training, Domain-Adaptive Pre-training, Continual Fine-Tuning. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2404.16789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16789)
- Chen, Sun, Ye, Li, Lin, "Beyond Static Models: An Evolving Framework for Continual Learning in Large Language Models across Training Stages" (arXiv 2603.12658; v2 dated 9 Aug 2026; a search listing shows an earlier title, "Continual Learning in Large Language Models: Methods, Challenges, and Opportunities"). Taxonomy: continual pre-training, continual fine-tuning, continual alignment, read through three knowledge-dynamics modes: collision (mitigated), isolation, fusion. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- What works (Chen et al.): rehearsal ("a strong baseline"; 5-15% prior-domain mixing); PEFT isolation ("the dominant design pattern" in CFT); sparse updates, which "consistently reduce forgetting without proportional sacrifices in plasticity". **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- What does not work or is limited (Chen et al.): pure regularisation at LLM scale; architecture-based methods at pre-training scale; most methods "prioritize stability over plasticity", and forward transfer "is rarely reported and even more rarely optimized". **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- Unsolved (Chen et al.): the "stability gap" (initial dip before recovery) "remains theoretically underexplained"; PEFT routing quality; continual alignment is "the most under-studied of the three stages", and "Preference-conflict arbitration therefore remains a largely open research direction"; most benchmarks use only ~5-15 tasks, and the authors call for longitudinal benchmarks with "long task sequences (50+ tasks)"; cross-stage interference, where gains at one stage "can be silently erased by the next" — "a structural challenge with no adequate solution yet". **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- Stated priorities (Chen et al.): a theory of forgetting at LLM scale (over-parameterisation, stability gap, domain-shift geometry); longitudinal benchmarks; and a "general CL agent" that combines retrieval, selective internalisation and continual alignment, whose central open problem is "when to retrieve, when to internalize, and when to re-align". They recommend semi-parametric designs in which the non-parametric part "can absorb fast factual updates" while parameters remain a stable competence store. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)
- Hou et al., "Continual Learning in Transition" (arXiv 2608.06216, 6 Aug 2026): open challenges named — (i) multi-faceted forgetting across carriers ("Under composite carriers, it has at least four faces": parameters, memory, context, skills/protocols); (ii) bidirectional parameter-harness transfer ("A complete bidirectional mechanism would have to answer when to consolidate capability into parameters"); (iii) "Coordinating writing and reclamation across carriers that move at different rates is a key open problem"; (iv) error accumulation in long-horizon agents; (v) the limit of verifiable rewards ("many real tasks, such as writing, consulting, and long-horizon planning, carry no explicit verifiable signal"); (vi) active forgetting ("external memory likewise requires an active forgetting mechanism"). **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2608.06216](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06216)
- Hou et al. on evaluation: static benchmarks measure what a model ships with rather than net capability gained during operation; harness and cross-axis methods are "far from mature in evaluation, stability, and reproducibility"; concrete evaluation protocols are left to future work. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2608.06216](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06216)
- Jiang et al. (arXiv 2606.24901, 12 Jun 2026) add an industrial critique: most existing research uses static benchmarks that miss real industrial needs, and repeated adaptation eroding model plasticity is listed first among core challenges. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2606.24901](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24901)
- Other surveys located but not opened: "Continual Learning for Generative AI: From LLMs to MLLMs and Beyond" (arXiv 2506.13045, June 2025), which states that continual learning for generative models remains challenging and underexplored; and "Recent Advances of Foundation Language Models-based Continual Learning: A Survey" (ACM Computing Surveys, published January 2025), which splits methods into offline and online CL with traditional, parameter-efficient, instruction-tuning and continual pre-training categories. **[search-summary]** — [arXiv 2506.13045](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13045v4); [ECNU record](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/recent-advances-of-foundation-language-models-based-continual-lea/)

### Inferences
- The surveys' "unsolved" lists map closely onto what a small controlled experiment can actually address: (a) the stability gap and forward/backward transfer, which are cheap to measure densely in a tiny model; (b) long task sequences (50+), which are feasible only at small scale; and (c) plasticity erosion under repeated adaptation, which is exactly what grow/prune mechanisms are meant to counteract.
- No survey read here treats dynamic neuron-level capacity (growth and pruning inside a shared network) as an established LLM method; architecture-based methods are discussed as module insertion (adapters, MoE experts, block expansion). In the portion of Chen et al. that was read, model growth, sleep/consolidation and neuroscience-inspired mechanisms do not appear in the challenges or opportunities sections.
- Model editing is conspicuously marginal in the 2026 surveys: Chen et al. mention editing only under alignment-stage "selective isolation", and Hou et al. do not discuss it at all. Combined with the Mirage and WikiBigEdit results, this suggests the field has stopped treating knowledge editing as a route to continual learning.

### Gaps
- Sections IX-A1 to IX-A3 of Chen et al. (the first three named challenges) fell on a page boundary and were not captured; the challenge list above is therefore incomplete for that survey.
- Only the abstract of Shi et al. (2404.16789) was read; its closing discussion of open questions is not recorded.
- Appendix tables of Hou et al. (cross-dimensional method profiles, benchmark catalogue) were not available in the extracted text.
- The surveys are narrative; none of those read provides a meta-analysis or a controlled re-run across method families, so "what works" is the authors' synthesis rather than a measured ranking.

## Key Question 4: Is there evidence on whether weight-based continual learning beats long-context or retrieval-based memory for adapting deployed models, and what are the arguments on each side?

### Takeaway
There is no settled answer and the direct evidence is thin, task-dependent and mostly from 2026 preprints. For injecting large volumes of *facts*, retrieval currently wins (WikiBigEdit; oracle RAG as the ceiling in SDFT). For retaining *procedural* knowledge across sessions, and for *structurally novel skills*, weight updates have won in the few direct comparisons (consolidation versus compaction; TTT versus ICL). The most careful 2026 comparison explicitly reports "no single winner", and several studies show that non-parametric memory relocates interference into retrieval rather than eliminating it.

### Cited Findings

#### Evidence favouring weight updates
- "Beyond Inference-Only Deployment: Comparing Weight-Based Consolidation Against Cascading Compaction" (Dennis, Shabahang, Guo, Patil; arXiv 2605.24657, 23 May 2026). Nightly reflect-synthesise-LoRA-fine-tune consolidation on a single consumer GPU versus repeated context summarisation, on ten software-development conversations with 1,146 test questions. Three compaction cycles retained 36.8 ± 3.0% of knowledge (floor 11.8% with no context; ceiling 90.1% with full context); consolidation retained 80.4 ± 1.3%, a 43.6 percentage-point gain (paired t(9) = 14.8, p < 0.001). Largest gains on procedural corrections (36.3% to 74.6%) and episodic project facts (31.5% to 78.2%). Caveats: n = 10 conversations, LLM-judged accuracy, unreviewed v1 preprint, base model not named in the abstract. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2605.24657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24657)
- Test-time training beats in-context learning on structurally novel tasks: up to 6x higher ARC accuracy than fine-tuned baselines, and +7.3 points over few-shot prompting on BBH (50.5% to 57.8%). In SEAL's ARC subset, ICL scored 0% where TTT-based methods scored 20-72.5%. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2411.07279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07279); [arXiv 2506.10943](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2)
- In-context continual learning has its own interference. A theoretical ICML 2026 paper (Li, Ding, Hu; arXiv 2605.28705, 27 May 2026) shows that when several tasks are placed sequentially in one prompt, standard attention causes inter-task interference and systematic bias, with a bias-variance-interference decomposition identifying when past in-context information provably hurts; "forgetting intrinsically arises from how the attention mechanism aggregates this history." Empirically on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (SST-2 then AG News in context), Task A accuracy fell from 0.934 to 0.472 with one Task-B example and was still 0.536 with 19. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2605.28705](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28705)
- External memory can hurt: raw-trajectory memory produced negative forward transfer (-9.5 and -7.5), and growing memory pools produced retrieval dilution with BWT of -10.0; the paper's conclusion is that memory "shift[s] its mechanism from parameter updates to retrieval dynamics". **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2604.27003](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27003)
- Survey position (Hou et al.): "Once written, however, parameters offer the most stable recall", whereas harness capability is "strongly coupled to context" and transfers less readily. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2608.06216](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06216)
- Publicly stated position (blog, 2 June 2025): Dwarkesh Patel argues "the fundamental problem is that LLMs don't get better over time the way a human would"; "You're stuck with the abilities you get out of the box"; RL fine-tuning exists but "it's just not a deliberate, adaptive process the way human learning is"; and context summaries are brittle — "Even Claude Code will often reverse a hard-earned optimization that we engineered together before I hit /compact." He gives a 50/50 date of 2032 for AI that learns on the job as well as a human for white-collar work. **[blog]** — [Dwarkesh Patel, "Why I don't think AGI is right around the corner"](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/timelines-june-2025)
- Publicly stated position (podcast, 26 Sep 2025): Richard Sutton defines continual learning as "learning during the normal interaction with the world", argues LLMs "are trying to get by without having a goal or a sense of better or worse", and says of on-the-job knowledge that "you're going to have to learn it along the way" because it "can't all have been put in in advance". **[blog/podcast transcript]** — [Dwarkesh Podcast, Richard Sutton episode](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/richard-sutton)

#### Evidence favouring retrieval, context, or non-parametric memory
- WikiBigEdit (ICML 2025): at 500K-edit scale, RAG "vastly outperforms specialized knowledge editing techniques (at higher inference cost)" and is the best approach when inference cost is unconstrained (78.64% rephrased, 66.16% persona); the paper highlights "continual finetuning coupled with model merging as a competitive alternative at an equivalent inference cost". **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2503.05683](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05683v2)
- In SDFT's knowledge-acquisition test, oracle RAG is the ceiling (91 / 100 / 100) and the best weight-based method only approaches it (SDFT 89 / 100 / 98; SFT 80 / 95 / 80; continued pretraining 9 / 37 / 7). **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2601.19897](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19897)
- In data-matched controlled settings, in-context learning generalised more flexibly than fine-tuning (for example to reversals and syllogistic inferences); fine-tuning caught up and surpassed ICL only when the fine-tuning data was augmented with in-context inferences. **[search-summary of abstract]** — [Lampinen et al., arXiv 2505.00661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)
- "Latent learning" (arXiv 2509.16189): parametric learning fails to capture information not relevant to the current task; a system with oracle retrieval of episodes generalises better across those challenges. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2509.16189](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16189)
- Publicly stated positions reported by a news outlet (Transformer, 22 Jan 2026): Dario Amodei said in an August 2025 interview that continual learning "is another of those problems that is not as difficult as it seems"; Sholto Douglas (Anthropic) predicted on X that "continual learning gets solved in a satisfying way" in 2026; Nathan Lambert called it "the ultimate algorithmic nerd snipe for AI researchers" and argued scaling current systems will suffice; Andrej Karpathy is reported as seeing the field as "three or four or five" big papers away. **[news; quotes not verified against originals]** — [Transformer, "Teaching AI to learn"](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/teaching-ai-to-continual-learning)
- Nathan Lambert's own post frames the gap as a systems/product problem rather than a learning-algorithm bottleneck: "This doesn't feel like a bottleneck, rather another product problem that we are going to solve." **[blog; search-summary]** — [Interconnects, "Contra Dwarkesh on Continual Learning"](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/contra-dwarkesh-on-continual-learning)
- Safety argument for frozen weights, as reported by the same news article: Yejin Choi (Davos 2026) warned that for models that keep learning after deployment, earlier safety tests "may not be valid anymore"; frozen weights also prevent users from corrupting a shared model through feedback. **[news]** — [Transformer, "Teaching AI to learn"](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/teaching-ai-to-continual-learning)

#### Evidence that the answer is "it depends on the carrier and the budget"
- "Where Should a Document Live: Context, Representations, or Parameters?" (Rakotonirina, Hardalov, Iglesias, de Gispert; arXiv 2609.17346, 15 Sep 2026) compares KV-cache representations (Cartridges, Compaction) against parametric methods (full fine-tuning, large MLP adapters) with ICL as reference on five knowledge-intensive benchmarks. Abstract: "no single winner". Cartridges are the most accurate injection method at nearly every storage budget, beating parametric methods by 10 points; Compaction matches Cartridges only at low compression and above 50x lags parametric methods by 10 points; in multi-document retrieval Cartridges are the only method matching ICL (leading parametric methods by 29 points and Compaction by 15). **Forgetting** is reported for Cartridges, full fine-tuning and large MLP adapters; Cartridges show a 6% drop on control benchmarks and 13% on coding. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2609.17346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17346)
- A search listing of the same paper additionally reports scores of 70.6 (Cartridges), 64.5 (LoRA), 62.2 (MLP adapters) and 59.8 (full fine-tuning). These figures were **not** in the abstract I read and are unverified. **[search-summary]** — [alphaXiv listing for 2609.17346](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.17346)
- Both the 2026 surveys recommend hybrids rather than a winner: retrieval to "absorb recent or provenance-sensitive knowledge without immediately overwriting model parameters", parameters for validated, stable competence. **[fetched]** — [Chen et al., arXiv 2603.12658](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)

### Inferences
- The evidence sorts by *what is being learned*. Declarative facts at volume: retrieval wins on accuracy and safety, at an inference-cost premium and with a multi-hop weakness. Procedural corrections and skills that must generalise beyond the stored wording: weight updates win in the direct comparisons available, but those comparisons are small.
- Neither side escapes interference. Weight-space methods overwrite; context-space methods suffer attention-level inter-task interference; memory-space methods suffer retrieval dilution; even KV-cache methods show measurable forgetting on control tasks. A claim that "memory avoids catastrophic forgetting" is not supported by the 2026 literature.
- The argument for weights is at bottom about *consolidation and compression* (stable recall without re-reading, cheap inference, generalisation through synthetic re-expression), while the argument for memory is about *reversibility, provenance and safety*. Those are different axes, which is why the surveys converge on "decide per item where it should live".
- For positioning a small weight-based experiment: the defensible claim is not that weight-based learning beats memory in general, but that the consolidation step (moving knowledge into parameters without eroding plasticity or old skills) is the specific piece every hybrid proposal still needs and nobody has solved.

### Gaps
- No large, peer-reviewed, like-for-like benchmark comparing continual weight updates, long context and retrieval on the *same* deployed-adaptation stream was found. The closest items are a ten-conversation preprint (2605.24657) and a document-injection study (2609.17346), neither peer reviewed as far as their arXiv pages indicate.
- The Amodei, Douglas, Karpathy and Choi quotes come from a single news article and were not checked against the original interviews or posts. I found no primary-source 2026 statement from Demis Hassabis or Sam Altman on this question; the Sutskever position (agreement that continual learning matters, Dwarkesh Podcast, November 2025) was located only through secondary summaries.
- "Long context vs. RAG for LLMs: An evaluation and revisits" (arXiv 2501.01880) and "Fine-tuning with RAG for Improving LLM Learning of New Skills" (arXiv 2510.01375) surfaced in search but were not opened.
- No evidence was found on the *economic* comparison (cost per retained item over a deployment lifetime) beyond WikiBigEdit's observation that RAG inference time at most doubles.

## Key Question 5: What small-scale or from-scratch transformer experiments on continual learning exist, and what did they find?

### Takeaway
The small-scale, from-scratch transformer literature on continual learning is sparse and scattered; I found a handful of relevant studies and none that combines a tiny transformer, an algorithmic task sequence, and neuron-level growth and pruning. The consistent findings are that tiny transformers forget under strict sequential training even when capacity is demonstrably sufficient, that replay or distillation fixes this, and that with replay small transformers shift toward in-context adaptation rather than weight storage.

### Cited Findings
- **Sufficient capacity does not prevent forgetting.** Xu, "Low-Dimensional Execution Manifolds in Transformer Learning Dynamics: Evidence from Modular Arithmetic Tasks" (arXiv 2602.10496, v2 13 Feb 2026). Attention-only transformers, embedding dimension 128 (each attention matrix 16,384 parameters), trained from random initialisation with SGD (lr 0.001, batch 64); task: sum of m marked operands mod C (C = 8 or 16) in length-32 sequences. Mixed training on m = 1..4 succeeds, but strict curriculum (m increased progressively) "leads to catastrophic forgetting" of smaller m, even though "the model has sufficient capacity to represent all task variants simultaneously"; the paper attributes this to path-dependent movement through fragmented solution regions. Forgetting is reported qualitatively only; layer count and total parameters are not stated; seed count is inconsistent within the paper (20 in Methods, 5 in a figure caption). **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2602.10496](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10496v2)
- **Distillation from the previous solution prevents forgetting in a grokking setup.** Singh, Belilovsky, Aljundi, "When Data Falls Short: Grokking Below the Critical Threshold" (arXiv 2511.04760, 6 Nov 2025; CCFM workshop). One-layer decoder-only transformer trained from scratch on modular addition/subtraction; distribution p1 uses P = 113, shifted distribution p2 uses P = 107. In continual pretraining from p1 to p2 without knowledge distillation, accuracy on p1 drops rapidly while p2 generalises quickly; with KD from the grokked p1 model, p1 accuracy stays near perfect — "KD thus prevented delayed generalization and preserved prior knowledge". Width and parameter counts are not given. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2511.04760](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04760v1)
- **With replay, a small transformer becomes an in-context few-shot learner after an initial plasticity dip.** Bornschein, Li, Rannen-Triki (Google DeepMind), "Transformers for Supervised Online Continual Learning" (arXiv 2403.01554, 3 Mar 2024). On a synthetic piecewise-stationary, task-agnostic stream (Split-EMNIST: 100 tasks, each a random 10-way classification from 47 classes), a 4-block transformer of width 256 on top of a 6-layer CNN shows first "a performance drop that is generally associated with loss of plasticity issues", then after roughly 20 tasks becomes a few-shot learner and keeps improving to at least task 100. "Without replay, the model usually does not learn to use in-context information effectively." On CLOC (37,093,769 images) average accuracy: experience replay 20%, ACM 26%, their model trained from scratch 67%, with MAE ViT-L features 70%, and 19% with attention removed. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2403.01554](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01554)
- **Replay plus a transformer removes loss of plasticity.** Wang, Chandra, Zhang, "Experience Replay Addresses Loss of Plasticity in Continual Learning" (arXiv 2503.20018, 25 Mar 2025): in continual regression, classification and policy-evaluation tasks, adding experience replay and processing the replayed data with Transformers makes loss of plasticity "disappear", without changing backpropagation, activation functions or regularisation; the authors conjecture (without proof) that in-context learning is the mechanism. A search summary states the comparison is against an MLP that does lose plasticity; the abstract does not confirm that detail. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2503.20018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20018)
- **Forgetting also occurs inside the context of small from-scratch transformers.** Li, Ding, Hu (arXiv 2605.28705, ICML 2026) train decoder-style GPT-2 models from scratch on linear regression (following Garg et al. 2022): a 12-layer / 8-head / 256-dim model and a tiny 3-layer / 2-head / 64-dim model (a search summary gives ~0.2M parameters for the tiny model). With several tasks concatenated in one prompt and parameters frozen, earlier tasks suffer more forgetting after later tasks are processed; small angle between task weight vectors gives positive transfer and larger angles give negative transfer; "Long context improves generalization only beyond a task-alignment threshold." On sparse linear regression, Task 5's normalised MSE reaches 0.9905 at M = 19. **[fetched]** — [arXiv 2605.28705](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28705)
- **A non-backprop local-learning alternative forgets less but learns much less.** Atmuri and Bhogarajula, "The Art of Not Forgetting: A Local Learning Architecture for Continual Learning" (arXiv 2607.26523, 29 Jul 2026): CMP (Cognitive Memory Primitive) uses sparse relational codes, a two-tier competitive memory and local updates; on a 15-domain, three-seed domain-incremental byte-level language-modeling protocol it shows "substantially lower backward transfer than a parameter-matched Transformer" trained with online EWC, but with a substantial single-domain accuracy gap relative to the Transformer and a null result on a vision benchmark. No numbers in the abstract. **[fetched, abstract only]** — [arXiv 2607.26523](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26523)
- **From-scratch continual pre-training at GPT-2 Medium scale.** TFGN reports a From-Scratch regime at ~398M parameters across six domains, including a 62.0% drop in held-out JavaScript perplexity after Python training (presented as forward transfer). ### Inferences
- The small-scale literature supports a specific, testable baseline expectation for a tiny transformer on a task sequence: strict sequential training will forget even when a jointly trained model of the same size solves everything. This makes "joint/mixed training at equal parameter count" the necessary upper-bound control, and makes capacity-based explanations of forgetting insufficient on their own; the Xu result points to optimisation path dependence rather than capacity shortage.
- Because replay and distillation each fix forgetting cheaply at this scale, a grow/prune mechanism needs to be compared against (a) a small replay buffer and (b) self-distillation from the previous checkpoint, not only against naive sequential fine-tuning and EWC. Otherwise a positive result will not be informative.
- Two confounds are specific to transformers and should be controlled: (1) in-context adaptation — a transformer given task examples in its context may "solve" continual learning without storing anything in weights (Bornschein et al.; Wang et al.), so the context design determines whether weight-level retention is being tested at all; and (2) spurious forgetting — performance may drop from misalignment rather than knowledge loss (Zheng et al.), so retention should also be measured after a brief re-alignment or with probes.
- Within the sources located, neuron-level growth and pruning in a tiny from-scratch transformer over an algorithmic or synthetic task sequence appears to be unoccupied territory. The nearest neighbours are depth stacking during pretraining at 7B scale, per-task module addition in PEFT, and expand-then-reset of low-rank experts in the Sleep paper. This is an inference from an absence of search hits across five differently phrased queries, not a demonstrated gap.
- Scale caution: several benign phenomena reported for LLMs (anticipatory recovery, robustness of pretrained models to forgetting) are stated to strengthen with size, so results from a tiny model should not be assumed to transfer upward, and vice versa.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that studies neuron-level grow-and-prune for continual learning in a small transformer trained from scratch on algorithmic task sequences. Searches for "dynamic architecture continual learning transformer" and "neurogenesis transformer catastrophic forgetting" were not run; the classical dynamic-architecture literature (progressive networks, dynamically expandable networks, continual backpropagation) is pre-2024 background and was out of scope for this note.
- Dohare et al. (Nature, 2024) on loss of plasticity and continual backpropagation, which is the most direct precedent for selective unit re-initialisation, could not be verified here (login redirect). - Quantitative forgetting numbers are missing for the two most on-point small-transformer studies: Xu (2602.10496) reports forgetting qualitatively, and Singh et al. (2511.04760) give curves and a single "about 92% to 100%" jump rather than a forgetting table.
- I found no small-scale study that measures *plasticity loss* (declining ability to learn new tasks) in a transformer across a long algorithmic task sequence without replay; the two plasticity papers found both include replay.
- "Too Big to Think: Capacity, Memorization, and Generalization in Pre-Trained Transformers" (arXiv 2506.09099; nanoGPT-based models on synthetic arithmetic and factual recall) studies capacity rather than continual learning and was not opened.
