# Fixed networks stiffen; growing ones stay unproven

Research from 2024 through late 2026 establishes both halves of the continual-learning problem firmly and leaves the brain-like remedy largely untested. Networks trained on a stream lose what they learned (catastrophic forgetting) and, separately, lose the ability to learn at all (loss of plasticity); the second now has direct evidence in GPT-style language models from **5M to 314M parameters**, where scale only delays the onset and the smallest models degrade first ([Hernandez-Garcia et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24752)). Adding and pruning neurons has support in separate pieces: neuron turnover restores plasticity in MLPs and CNNs, freezing old parameters stops forgetting when the task is identified, and saturation-triggered growth matches well-sized fixed networks with fewer parameters in small MLPs. Yet in the cleanest size-matched tests a grown network **ties or slightly trails a fixed network of the same final size**, growth's accuracy wins come mainly under distribution shift and are worth roughly one to six points, and no study located combines on-demand feed-forward growth, protection of old neurons and pruning in a transformer learning a task sequence. Language-model practice has gone a different way, adding whole modules (a LoRA branch, adapter or expert per task) and replaying old data, with neuron-level growth nearly absent. The planned tiny-transformer experiment therefore occupies a real gap, and the literature is specific about what makes its result informative: a fixed baseline with the same final size and the same protection mechanism, a joint-training upper bound, cheap reference baselines, more than three seeds for the close comparisons, and measurements that separate lost knowledge from lost readout and forgetting from stiffening. Much of the newest evidence is unreviewed 2026 preprints, and coverage of September to November 2026 is thin, so the frontier claims are provisional.

## Every network studied eventually stiffens, and the smallest transformers stiffen first

Loss of plasticity is the gradual decline in a network's ability to learn anything new, and it is a different failure from forgetting. The reference demonstration is Dohare et al. in *Nature*: under ordinary backpropagation on a long stream of Permuted MNIST tasks, up to 25% of units were dead after 800 tasks, and with Adam the dead fraction plateaued near 60%; on Continual ImageNet, early tasks reached up to 88% test accuracy and plasticity was substantially gone by task 2,000 at every step size ([Dohare et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11338828/)). Their remedy, continual backpropagation (CBP), is neuron turnover at fixed size: it keeps reinitialising a small fraction of low-utility units, scores utility as a running average of activation times outgoing-weight magnitude, gives each new unit zero outgoing weights, and protects it from removal until it matures ([Dohare et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11338828/)). That is the mainstream literature's nearest analogue of pruning and adding neurons, and it sets the question for this section: where does turnover work, and where does it stop?

The explanation of why networks stiffen has moved from single causes to several partly independent ones. Lyle et al. decompose the phenomenon into separate mechanisms (shifting preactivation distributions that create dead and near-linear units, growth of parameter norms, the scale of regression targets, and the abruptness of the shift) and show that fixing one leaves the others in place ([Lyle et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18762)). The statistics people usually monitor are now read as symptoms. A survey of more than 50 mitigation strategies in deep reinforcement learning treats dormant and saturated neurons as signs of reduced capacity, not root causes ([Klein et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04832)), and in language models neither dormant MLP units nor weight magnitude tracked the loss consistently: the 12M model stiffened most severely with no dormant-unit trend, while the 53M model had more than 95% dormant units in one layer ([Hernandez-Garcia et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24752)). Theory from 2025 and 2026 looks for one geometric fact underneath. Joudaki et al. prove that gradient descent becomes trapped on manifolds of frozen (saturated) and cloned (duplicated) units, and that Adam stays trapped on the cloned kind ([Joudaki et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.00304v2)); Prakash et al. argue that the Hessian spectrum collapses at the start of a new task before other symptoms appear ([Prakash et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22335)); Rosseau et al. recast plasticity as the conditioning of the empirical neural tangent kernel and propose keeping every layer close to an isometry ([Rosseau et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09762)). The shared reading is that a stiff network has too few usable gradient directions at the moment a new task arrives. None of these theories has an independent replication, and the diagnostics they displace are demonstrably unreliable: Wang et al. construct checkpoints with high representation rank and high kernel rank on which gradient descent makes zero progress ([Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.09044)).

For this project the decisive 2026 result is that transformers and language models are not exempt. Hernandez-Garcia, Figliolia and Millidge trained pre-norm GPT-style decoders with AdamW and weight decay 0.1 on a repeating cycle of eight languages and probed plasticity on a held-out ninth. **All eight model sizes eventually lost plasticity**, with onset at task 6 for the 5M and 12M models, task 16 at 27M, task 54 at 53M and task 118 at 314M; a fitted power law with exponent about 0.83 (eight data points) extrapolates to roughly 360 tasks for a 1B model, so scale delays the problem sublinearly without removing it ([Hernandez-Garcia et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24752)). The smallest model, 4 layers at width 320, degraded immediately after its first cycle, and stationary training on a mixture of all eight languages also produced the loss in the three smallest models ([Hernandez-Garcia et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24752)). That second observation cuts against the argument that plasticity loss is mainly a benchmark artefact, an argument with real support: Liu and Mou find that abrupt switches cause accuracy drops after about 20 tasks on Random MNIST and 12 on a cipher task, while interpolating between tasks preserves near-perfect accuracy ([Liu and Mou 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09234v2)). The evidence supports a middle position: abruptness sets the speed, the loss still occurs without it, and the smallest models are the most exposed. No mitigation has been tested in that language-model setting; the nearest evidence is correlational, with 0.5B to 1B models fine-tuning best downstream when pretrained at a weight decay of 1.0, ten times the common default ([Han et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11137v2)).

Smaller transformer studies locate the damage and show which fixes fail. On Permuted Shakespeare, a single-layer decoder trained through 500 character-permutation tasks ended at an average loss of 3.01 with no intervention; resetting MLP units barely helped (CBP 2.94, ReDo 2.81), whereas L2 regularisation reached 0.31 and resets combined with L2 reached 0.26, and the authors conclude that attention weights must be regularised as well ([Farias and Jozefiak](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20098v3)). In a ViT-B/16 on a 200-task stream the feed-forward module was the structural bottleneck, with the lowest effective rank, the fastest weight growth and rising dormant units, yet CBP performed at roughly baseline (57.4 / 66.2 / 67.1 against 57.2 / 66.5 / 70.9 for the unmodified model across three task counts) ([Sun et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07787v1)). Unit reinitialisation is also reported unstable with layer normalisation and in networks as narrow as 10 units per layer ([Hernandez-Garcia et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.00212v1)). The feed-forward block is therefore the right place to look in a transformer, and resetting its units alone is, on current evidence, not a sufficient intervention there.

Across independent comparisons the dependable recipes control weight norms and restore unit activity together, and simple ones hold their own. The table shows the pattern in one benchmark suite trained with Adam.

Layer normalisation alone helps on only one of the four problems, and every positive result for it elsewhere pairs it with weight decay or a pull toward initialisation; layer norm with weight decay was the most robust configuration in Lyle et al.'s tests ([Lyle et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18762)). L2 Init, a one-hyperparameter penalty, matched or beat CBP on four of five problems ([Kumar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.11958v3)), and the reinforcement-learning survey concludes that general regularisation often outperforms purpose-built interventions ([Klein et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04832)). The hidden cost is sensitivity: moving L2 Init, shrink-and-perturb or CBP one order of magnitude from its best setting cut accuracy by at least 72 points in the same suite ([Farias and Jozefiak](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20098v3)). Growth combined with pruning appears as a plasticity method only in reinforcement learning: Neuroplastic Expansion starts agents at about a quarter of full size, grows the connections with the largest gradients, prunes dormant neurons under a per-layer cap, and outperforms reset, ReDo, layer-norm and plasticity-injection baselines on MuJoCo and DeepMind Control, with margins reported in figures only ([Liu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07994v3)).

Measurement is where the field has become most self-critical. The only quantity treated as ground truth is a direct probe: train on a new or held-out task for a fixed budget and compare with a freshly initialised network ([Klein et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04832)). Internal statistics rank checkpoints poorly by comparison. In one 2026 test a gradient-based readiness score ordered checkpoint pairs correctly 98.7% and 90.6% of the time on two benchmarks, effective rank managed 69.8% and 85.8%, and the fraction of active neurons 67.1% and 34.6% ([Wang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.09044)). Restoring trainability also fails to restore generalisation: four methods that revived training-loss convergence left warm-start test accuracy on CIFAR-10 between 0.678 and 0.705 against a 0.704 baseline ([Lee et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.02596v1)).

## Forgetting follows task similarity and training regime, and much of it is recoverable

Theory from 2024 to 2026, built mostly on linear models, teacher-student setups and infinite-width limits, says forgetting has no single dial. In heavily overparameterised models, tasks of intermediate similarity forget most ([Goldfarb et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12617)), and the worst combination is similar inputs with dissimilar readouts, which damages both transfer and retention ([Hiratani 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20236)). Width helps only insofar as it makes training lazier, that is, reduces feature learning, and the best operating point is an intermediate level of feature learning that depends on how non-stationary the tasks are ([Graldi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16884)); widening a fixed feed-forward network reduces forgetting with diminishing returns ([Guha and Lakshman 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06398)). Order matters too. Random task ordering alone drives forgetting toward zero in long sequences of jointly solvable linear tasks ([Evron et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04579)), and placing dissimilar tasks next to each other improved task-incremental results for MLPs and CNNs ([Li and Hiratani 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03350)). Step size is a further confound: in a from-scratch transformer, larger fine-tuning learning rates deepened the transient collapse of old-fact recall ([Palit et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08718)). No theory located for this period addresses finite attention models trained from scratch on sequential tasks, so each of these results reaches a tiny transformer by analogy only.

On methods, the verified evidence supports a ranking that depends on the scenario. The 2024 review by van de Ven, Soures and Kudithipudi finds replay important and perhaps necessary for class-incremental and large problems, parameter regularisation such as EWC effective in task- and domain-incremental settings but weak at learning boundaries between classes never seen together, and parameter isolation free of forgetting only when task identity is supplied ([van de Ven et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05175)). A growing network that freezes old neurons is a form of parameter isolation, so this last point governs how its results generalise. Replay is not unconditionally safe: in overparameterised linear regression forgetting is non-monotonic in the number of replayed samples, and some sample choices raise it ([Mahdaviyeh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04377)). Baselines are also easy to weaken by accident. Several incompatible Fisher-information computations circulate for EWC, and many reported EWC results are improvable by changing that one choice ([van de Ven 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11756)). The broadest reproducibility check is sobering: when hyperparameters were tuned on one dataset and frozen before evaluation on another, **most state-of-the-art methods failed to reproduce their reported performance across more than 8,000 class-incremental experiments** ([Cha and Cho 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09066)).

The most important conceptual shift of the period is that a large share of measured forgetting is loss of access, not erasure. Language models fine-tuned on documents in a fixed cyclic order recover more than half of their initial forgetting on a document before seeing it again, an effect that needs width of at least 512 and depth of at least 8 blocks to appear in models trained from scratch ([Yang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09613)). Zheng et al. attribute much of the drop in language-model continual learning to disrupted task alignment, and report that freezing the bottom layers helps substantially ([Zheng et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13453)). Palit et al. decompose forgetting in an 8-layer from-scratch transformer into a fast, shared and reversible collapse followed by slow, permanent erosion of individual facts; in a pretrained 1B model, removing a single direction from each weight update largely removes the collapse ([Palit et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08718)). In an unreviewed vision study, accuracy on earlier tasks fell from 54.8% to 0% while linear probes retained about 76% of the representational information and retraining only the classifier restored 75.7% of the original performance ([Trivedi and Melwani 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06032)). Every one of these sources also reports a residue that does not come back, and their figures use different models, tasks and definitions, so no comparable estimate of the superficial fraction exists. The practical consequence is that accuracy alone overstates knowledge loss, and two systems with identical final accuracy can differ in what they have permanently lost.

Direct evidence in small from-scratch transformers is sparse but consistent. An attention-only transformer of width 128 trained on modular-sum tasks solved every variant under mixed training and forgot the earlier ones under a strict curriculum, despite demonstrably sufficient capacity; the author attributes this to path dependence, not capacity shortage ([Xu 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10496v2)). A one-layer decoder moved from one modular-arithmetic distribution to another lost the first rapidly, and distillation from the previous solution kept it near perfect ([Singh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04760v1)). With replay, a 4-block transformer showed an initial plasticity dip and then, after about 20 tasks, turned into an in-context few-shot learner; without replay it did not learn to use in-context information ([Bornschein et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01554)). None of these studies involves growth, and none matches the planned design.

The standard metrics are all functions of one matrix a(k, j), the accuracy on task j after training through task k: final average accuracy, the forgetting measure (best earlier accuracy minus final accuracy, averaged over old tasks), backward transfer, intransigence against a jointly trained reference, and forward transfer ([Wang et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00487)). Two non-equivalent definitions of forward transfer circulate, one zero-shot against random initialisation ([Lopez-Paz and Ranzato 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)) and one after training against a from-scratch single-task model, so the choice must be stated. Algebraically, final average accuracy equals mean learning accuracy plus scaled backward transfer, which splits any headline number into a plasticity term and a stability term. Evaluating only at task boundaries hides the stability gap, a sharp transient drop in old-task performance at each switch that appears across replay, distillation and regularisation methods ([De Lange et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13452)).

## At equal final size growth ties fixed networks; its edge appears mainly under shift

Growth research in this period falls into three families. The first expands transformers with function-preserving operators on a fixed schedule, mainly to cut pretraining cost: depth-wise stacking produced a 7B model that reached a conventionally trained model's loss with 194B tokens in place of 300B, a 54.6% speedup ([Du et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15319)). The second adds neurons when a trigger fires and exists only at MLP and CNN scale. The third makes size a gradient-trained quantity. A caution applies to all three: how well a growth step preserves the function at initialisation is a misleading predictor of final quality, and early post-growth training dynamics predict better ([Karp et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02469)).

The closest precedent for on-demand neuron growth with protection is NORACL, which grows 1- and 2-layer MLPs under online EWC. A layer grows only when two signals fire together: its effective dimension has saturated relative to the end of the previous task, and a fresh Fisher estimate on the current task exceeds the layer's accumulated Fisher average. New neurons receive orthogonal incoming weights and zero outgoing weights, the optimiser is re-initialised, and a three-epoch cooldown follows ([Raghunathan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27031)). Its ablations are the only direct evidence on trigger design: either signal alone was worse, and **fixed, scheduled and loss-based triggers were at least 1.9 points worse** than the combined saturation trigger ([Raghunathan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27031)). Where neurons were added depended on the task stream, with dissimilar tasks growing the first layer and similar tasks the second, and total size varied about twofold between streams (roughly 49k parameters on Permuted MNIST against 23k on Binary Split MNIST) ([Raghunathan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27031)). That is the best available evidence that grown size tracks what is learned and not merely how many tasks arrived. For transformers, the nearest work is Google's "Grow, Don't Overwrite", which doubles the feed-forward width of a pretrained 1B model by copying and halving weights, trains only the new weights, and holds original-domain accuracy within about two points where full fine-tuning falls to near zero on large domain shifts, at a cost of roughly 60% more parameters; it tests one new task at a time and never a sequence, uses no trigger, and includes no dense baseline at the expanded size ([Adila et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08647v1)).

Newly added neurons integrate badly, and the literature disagrees on how to initialise them. Lillo and Cheney's central finding is that newborn units are "forward-active yet backward-starved": they reach near-incumbent activation while receiving much weaker gradients, and fixes to the optimiser, insertion rule or activation improve integration without reliably improving final quality ([Lillo and Cheney 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15435)). A separate group builds a method around the same observation that added neurons fail to adjust to a trained network and stay inactive ([Chatzis et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18842)). On initialisation, zero-initialised growth underperformed weight copying in the pretrained language model ([Adila et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08647v1)), orthogonal incoming weights with zero outgoing weights beat alternatives by at least 1.3 points in small MLPs ([Raghunathan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27031)), and the invariant-manifold theory warns that exact duplicates sit on a manifold that neither SGD nor Adam leaves without noise ([Joudaki et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.00304v2)). The best scheme is setting-dependent.

The question that matters most for the project's thesis is whether growing on demand beats starting at the final size. The evidence is thin and mixed.

The pattern is consistent. In stationary training with matched size and budget, growth does not improve final accuracy outside toy-scale regression, and it costs optimisation steps; the NGN author states that the benefit is automatic size selection, not better performance at the selected size ([Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.27291)). Under non-stationarity growth sometimes helps, and Lillo and Cheney's rewinding test shows that the advantage comes from the training trajectory and not from a better final architecture ([Lillo and Cheney 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15435)). The plausible mechanism is that fresh units restore plasticity while a large network consolidated from the start stiffens or wastes capacity; this remains a hypothesis, and NORACL's own size-matched control sits inside its error bars. Growth also has a cost in later adaptability: in the same structural-plasticity study, growing across all residual layers ended at 41.9% on a new-task adaptability test against 51.3% for the dense network ([Lillo and Cheney 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15435)). Published wins for growth are mostly on compute to reach a loss or on parameters actually used, not on accuracy at equal parameters.

Pruning has the stronger record of the two operations. Under repeated-shift benchmarks, pruning was generally the strongest or near-strongest structural method, with growth competitive but more regime-sensitive ([Lillo and Cheney 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15435)). Dynamic sparse training, which prunes and regrows connections at fixed parameter count, now reaches language-model scale: at 70% sparsity a 1B LLaMA trained with a brain-network-inspired regrowth rule reached perplexity 14.53 against 14.62 dense, random regrowth reached 16.37, and gradient-based RigL collapsed to 149.17, a comparison run by the winning method's authors, who attribute RigL's result partly to numerical precision ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19107v4)). In multi-task reinforcement learning, gradual pruning and sparse evolutionary training reduced neuron dormancy and representational collapse and were competitive with explicit plasticity interventions ([Todorov et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06871)). No study located applies prune-and-regrow training to continual learning in a transformer or language model.

The biological framing in this literature is mostly motivational. The neurogenesis-inspired papers converge on a three-part recipe (add units when existing ones saturate, consolidate or freeze what old tasks use, recycle inactive units), and each part has separate small-scale support, yet no paper validates all three together outside reinforcement learning, none shows neurogenesis improving continual learning in a transformer, and NORACL explicitly leaves attention models to future work ([Raghunathan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27031)). No neuroscience-grounded quantitative comparison, such as matching biological neurogenesis rates, was found.

## Language models add modules and replay data; neuron-level growth is nearly absent

At language-model scale the dependable methods either re-present old data or leave old parameters untouched. A 2026 survey organised by training stage calls rehearsal a strong baseline for continual pretraining, with **5 to 15% of prior-domain data mixed in** consistently preserving general capability, and calls parameter-efficient isolation (a frozen base with per-task LoRA modules) the dominant pattern in continual fine-tuning ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)). LoRA itself learns less and forgets less than full fine-tuning ([Biderman et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673)). Methods that try to protect shared weights analytically are the ones reported to fail. In the survey's illustrative T5-Large comparison EWC averaged 30.75 on performance with a forgetting rate of 28.41, against a forgetting rate of 9.13 for an orthogonal-subspace LoRA method, and a plain sequential LoRA swung 21 points between two task orders ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)). Knowledge editing fares worse: on a stream of 502,382 real Wikidata edits, the classic locate-then-edit methods collapsed within a few hundred updates and retrieval augmentation beat every specialised editor by a wide margin ([Thede et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05683v2)); under realistic evaluation single-edit success fell to 38.5% from a previously reported 96.8% ([Yang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11177)).

Growth exists in this literature almost entirely as module addition. One line adds a gated LoRA branch per task, which scales linearly with tasks ([Liang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15424)); another adds adapters only when a distribution-shift detector fires and claims sub-linear growth ([Wang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18886)); a third allocates LoRA experts across layers under a parameter budget ([Ge et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11672)). The survey names the price: once capacity is added per task, selecting the right capacity at inference without task labels becomes the bottleneck, and it judges architecture-based methods underused at pretraining scale because of the cost of inserting and warming up new modules ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)). The one study linking growth to forgetting at scale concerns growth during pretraining, not growth in response to new tasks: a 7B model pretrained by layer stacking forgot less under sequential fine-tuning than a conventionally trained twin (6.8 against 11.9 on reading comprehension, 7.3 against 8.2 on reasoning), an improvement its authors describe as modest, from a single grown model ([Süalp and Rezaei 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01213)). The closest analogue of a grow-then-prune cycle is Google's "sleep" method, which adds a low-rank expert before consolidation and resets earlier low-rank parameters afterwards, a step the authors compare to synaptic pruning; removing the expansion costs about one point on mathematics benchmarks, and its continual-learning results appear in figures only ([Behrouz et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03979v1)).

The prominent new framings of 2025 and 2026 share a structure and rest on thin evidence. Nested Learning recasts a model as nested optimisation problems updating at different frequencies and proposes the HOPE architecture ([Behrouz et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24695)); Google's announcement gives no numerical scores and no continual-learning results ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/introducing-nested-learning-a-new-ml-paradigm-for-continual-learning/)), no independent replication was found, and the lead author told a reporter he doubts the work is sufficient ([Transformer](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/teaching-ai-to-continual-learning)). MIT's self-adaptation line has the model generate its own training signal. SEAL raises no-context question answering on a new passage to 47.0%, from 33.5% when fine-tuning on the passage alone, and reports that performance on earlier tasks declines as self-edits accumulate ([Zweiger et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2)). Its successor, self-distillation fine-tuning, learns a science question-answering skill while holding prior capability at 64.5 against a base of 65.5, where ordinary fine-tuning drops to 53.4, at about 2.5 times the compute ([Shenfeld et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19897)). The recurring design is a fast, volatile store plus a slow, stable one with an explicit consolidation step between them, and the unsolved part is the consolidation policy: what to move, when, and into which capacity.

Whether weights are the right place to learn at all is contested, and the evidence sorts by what is being learned. For facts at volume retrieval wins ([Thede et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05683v2)). For procedural knowledge across sessions, a small preprint found nightly LoRA consolidation retained 80.4% against 36.8% for repeated context summarisation, over ten conversations ([Dennis et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24657)). The most careful comparison of context, cached representations and parameters reports no overall winner, and measurable forgetting in cached representations as well as in parametric methods ([Rakotonirina et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17346)). Memory does not escape interference either: placing a second task in the prompt cut first-task accuracy from 0.934 to 0.472 in a 1.5B model ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28705)), and a growing external memory produced backward transfer of minus 10 through retrieval dilution ([Hu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27003)). Both 2026 surveys recommend hybrids and identify the missing piece as a rule for when to consolidate knowledge into parameters ([Hou et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06216)). That is the defensible positioning for a weight-based growth experiment: it studies the consolidation step every hybrid still needs, not a replacement for memory.

## The experiment fills a real gap; its controls decide what the result means

*Note added 2026-11-19: this section was written against the first draft of the design. The design has since adopted several of the recommendations below: a small-replay reference arm, ten seeds, task order shuffled per seed, inputs that never repeat within an example, settings tuned on a separate stream and then locked, evaluation of old tasks during training, a marginal-cost metric, and a 50-task long-stream run. Distillation and EWC reference arms, relearning-speed measurements, and alternative growth triggers and pruning scores remain out of the first version.*

No paper located runs the planned design: a tiny transformer trained from scratch on a task sequence, adding feed-forward neurons on demand, protecting old ones, pruning the unused, and compared with fixed-size models. NORACL covers triggered growth with protection in MLPs, "Grow, Don't Overwrite" covers frozen-old feed-forward growth in a pretrained transformer for one task, and NGN covers feed-forward growth in a 2-layer transformer under stationary training. This is an absence across several differently phrased searches, not a proof that no such paper exists. The paragraphs below apply the literature to the draft design in this repository (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-11-19-continual-growth-experiment-design.md`), which freezes all parameters after each task, trains only newly added neurons, and identifies the task with a tag in the prompt.

### Freezing will carry the retention result, so growth must earn its keep elsewhere

The literature predicts the headline outcome with some confidence. Fixed-size models trained sequentially will forget heavily even with spare capacity, as the width-128 curriculum result shows ([Xu 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10496v2)), and a stream that reuses the same input symbols under different tags with conflicting outputs sits near the worst case identified by theory, similar inputs with dissimilar readouts ([Hiratani 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20236)). A model that freezes everything old will retain almost everything: frozen-old growth held retention within about two points in the pretrained-transformer study ([Adila et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08647v1)), and isolation avoids forgetting whenever task identity is available ([van de Ven et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05175)). Because the tag supplies task identity at test time, the experiment is task-incremental and must be labelled that way; its retention result says nothing about the class-incremental case, where the same review finds isolation insufficient. It also follows that low forgetting in the growing model is evidence for freezing, which is parameter isolation as practised since progressive networks ([Rusu et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)) and PackNet ([Mallya and Lazebnik 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05769)), and not evidence for growth. Hard freezing forgoes backward transfer as well, so the soft alternative (old neurons regularised, not frozen, as in NORACL) is the natural second experiment, and the two styles have not been compared head-to-head in any growth paper found.

### The pre-allocated baseline is the comparison nobody has published

Growth can only be credited with what a fixed network with the same protection fails to do. The draft's pre-allocated arm, a fixed network of the grown model's final width with one frozen block per task, is exactly the control this review could not find in any 2024 to 2026 paper, and it is the most valuable arm in the design. Expect a near tie on accuracy: NORACL's size-matched control differed by 0.9 points inside its error bars, and NGN found fixed networks equal or slightly better. Growth's defensible advantage is that it needs no oracle to size the network or its blocks, so results should report three things separately: accuracy at equal parameters, steps to reach it, and parameters actually used. Three seeds resolve the freeze-versus-no-freeze contrast, which is tens of points, and cannot resolve growth against pre-allocation, where published effects are about one point; NORACL used 5 seeds and NGN 10. The fixed baseline also deserves several widths, since width reduces forgetting with diminishing returns ([Guha and Lakshman 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06398)) and a wider network forgets less partly by training more lazily ([Graldi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16884)).

The draft excludes replay and regularisation baselines on the grounds that the thesis concerns structure alone. The literature's recurring failure mode is a new method that beats only weak comparators: distillation from the previous checkpoint kept old-task accuracy near perfect in a one-layer transformer ([Singh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04760v1)), small amounts of replay often suffice ([van de Ven et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05175)), and one-hyperparameter penalties match specialised plasticity methods ([Kumar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.11958v3)). Adding a small-replay arm, a distillation arm and an EWC arm with its Fisher computation stated, as reference lines and not as parts of the method, converts the result from a demonstration into a comparison. If they stay out, the claim must be scoped to "structure alone, with no old data, suffices here" and never "structure is competitive". Hyperparameters for every arm should be tuned on a separate task stream and then frozen, the protocol under which most published methods failed to reproduce ([Cha and Cho 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09066)), and task order should vary across seeds because order provably changes forgetting.

### Eight tasks measure forgetting, not the ability to keep learning

The thesis has two halves, retaining the old and continuing to learn the new, and the second is a claim about plasticity over long horizons. Plasticity loss took roughly 6 to 20 tasks to appear in small models under abrupt switches, and the standard benchmark suites run for hundreds to thousands of tasks, so an eight-task stream tests forgetting and little else. Small scale is where long streams are affordable, and the 2026 survey's call for sequences of 50 or more tasks is one a tiny model can answer ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12658)). In a freeze-and-grow model the new neurons are always fresh, so stiffening cannot show up as dead units; it will show up as the frozen substrate becoming a poorer foundation, which is the trade-off conceded by work that grows transformers on a frozen base ([Bochkov 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07129)). Two transformer-specific findings sharpen this. Plasticity damage concentrates in the feed-forward block, which favours growing there, but in the single-layer decoder study nothing worked until attention weights were handled too ([Farias and Jozefiak](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20098v3)). Attention that is frozen after a two-task base phase cannot degrade and cannot adapt, and the pretrained-model precedent for frozen attention started from a 1B model with general features, not a from-scratch model that has seen two lookup tables. Learning accuracy on late tasks, reported per task, is the main risk to watch.

### Mechanics to copy and traps to avoid

| Design element | What the literature shows | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Growth when the loss stalls | Loss-based, fixed and scheduled triggers were at least 1.9 points worse than a combined saturation trigger in MLPs ([NORACL](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27031)) | Keep the stall trigger as the simple default, log effective dimension per layer, and run a saturation trigger as an ablation |
| New neurons with near-zero output weights | Zero fan-out leaves old outputs untouched, but newborn units are gradient-starved ([Lillo and Cheney](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15435)), and copying beat zero-initialisation in a pretrained model ([Adila et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08647v1)) | Log per-neuron gradient norms for new units and treat initialisation as an ablation axis |
| Pruning by activation times output weight | This is CBP's utility score; weight-level work found gradient-based utility best and magnitude utility largely ineffective ([Hernandez-Garcia et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.00212v1)) | Compare against a gradient-based pruning criterion |
| Optimiser recreated at each growth event | NORACL re-initialises the optimiser and waits three epochs; LACE waits 60 steps ([LACE](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28611v1)) | Keep the reset and enforce a cooldown before the trigger can fire again |
| Weight decay on trainable neurons | Norm control is the most reproducible plasticity aid; layer norm alone is insufficient ([Farias and Jozefiak](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20098v3)) | Apply decay or a pull toward initialisation to new neurons only, with frozen values exempt |
| Task examples visible in context | Transformers can adapt in context without storing anything in weights ([Bornschein et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01554)) | Check that no answer is recoverable from the prompt, for example when an input symbol repeats within a sequence |

One inference from first principles belongs beside the table: with output weights at exactly zero, a new neuron's incoming weights receive no gradient until its output weights have moved, so near-zero is preferable to zero and the first steps after growth will be slow.

### Measure the full matrix, relearning speed and a matched fresh-start control

The measurement stack follows directly from the critiques above. Log the full a(k, j) matrix and evaluate old tasks during training on each new task, not only at its end, since transient collapse at task switches is invisible at boundaries and new neurons firing on old inputs is the draft's own stated risk. For every arm that forgets, add two cheap measurements that separate erased knowledge from lost readout: steps to relearn an old task compared with learning it from scratch, and accuracy after retraining only the output layer on frozen features. For plasticity, the trusted measure is a fixed-budget probe on a held-out task against a freshly initialised model, and when width changes during training that control must be resized to match, or capacity change is confounded with plasticity change. Dormant fraction, effective rank and weight norm are worth logging as descriptive covariates and nothing more. Report normalisation placement and learning rate, since both change the shape of forgetting curves. Benign effects seen in large models, such as anticipatory recovery, need width and depth a tiny model lacks, so neither their absence here nor any result here transfers upward without a scaling study. Finally, the numbers quoted in this report were extracted from abstracts and HTML pages by automated readers and several come from unreviewed preprints; each should be checked against the paper before it is cited in a publication.

## Conclusion

The idea that a model can keep learning the way a brain does, by adding and pruning neurons and growing as it learns, separates into three claims with very different support. Turnover preserves the ability to learn: well supported in MLPs and CNNs, fragile in transformers, where regularising weights has so far beaten replacing units. Protection preserves what was learned: well supported, but it is parameter isolation under another name and depends on knowing the task. Sizing on demand makes the network as large as its knowledge requires: this is the least tested claim, resting on one MLP study, and it is the only one a growth experiment can uniquely address. The experiment's most informative outputs are therefore not its forgetting score but the comparison with a pre-allocated network under identical freezing, and the curve of capacity against facts stored.

That curve also offers a measure of plasticity suited to systems that grow. A network that only adds fresh neurons never shows dead units, so stiffening has to be read from marginal cost: if the neurons or steps needed per new fact rise as the stream lengthens, the frozen substrate is aging even while accuracy holds. A flat marginal cost over fifty tasks would be the strongest small-scale evidence available that structural growth sustains learning; a rising one would show the frozen-substrate trade-off at its smallest scale. Either result speaks to the open problem the language-model field has converged on, which is how to consolidate new knowledge into parameters without eroding either old skills or future learning, and a tiny model that grows and prunes is the cheapest place to study that operation in isolation.
